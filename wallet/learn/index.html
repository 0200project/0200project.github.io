What a blockchain is

A public record of who owns what, kept by thousands of computers at once.

A blockchain is a shared record of who owns what. Thousands of computers around the world keep a copy, and they check each new entry against the rules before adding it. Because everyone has the same copy, no single company can quietly change it.

When you send crypto, you aren’t moving a file from one place to another. You’re adding a new line to that shared record: this amount now belongs to that address. Once the line is added, it stays there for good, and anyone can look it up.

Coins and tokens

Coins run their own network. Tokens ride on someone else’s.

A coin belongs to its own blockchain. Bitcoin (BTC) lives on the Bitcoin network, Ether (ETH) on Ethereum, SOL on Solana. Coins are also what you pay fees with on their network.

A token is built on top of someone else’s blockchain. USDC, for example, is a token that runs on Ethereum, Base, Solana and others. Many different tokens can live on one network.

A stablecoin is a token designed to stay at one dollar. USDC and USDT are the best known. Each is backed by the company that issues it, so it’s only as steady as that company.

What a wallet holds

Your crypto lives on the blockchain. Your wallet holds the key to it.

This is the part most people get backwards: your coins are never inside your wallet. They’re on the blockchain. A wallet holds keys, the secret that lets you move what the blockchain says is yours.

Think of the blockchain as a wall of glass lockers anyone can see into. Your address is your locker’s number. Your key is the only thing that opens it. Lose the app and keep the key, and you still have your crypto. Keep the app and lose the key, and you don’t.

Your recovery phrase

12 or 24 words. Whoever has them has your money.

When you create a wallet, you get a list of 12 or 24 ordinary words. That list is your key written in a form people can copy down. Type those words into any compatible wallet app, and your crypto is back.

  • Whoever has the words has the money. There’s no password reset and no bank to call.
  • Lose them and it’s gone for good. No one can recover them for you, not even the wallet’s makers.
  • Write them on paper and keep it somewhere safe. Never type them into a website, a chat or an email, and never photograph them.

No real support team will ever ask for your words. Anyone who does is trying to steal from you.

Addresses and networks

Right address, right network. Get either wrong and the money can be lost.

An address is where crypto gets sent, a long string like 0x1234…abcd. It’s safe to share, like an account number.

The catch is that the same token can exist on several networks. USDC on Ethereum and USDC on Solana are different things that happen to share a name. If you send on one network to an address that only works on another, the money can end up somewhere you can’t reach.

Before you send, check two things: the address, and that you and the person receiving are on the same network.

Fees

Every move costs a small fee, paid in the network’s own coin.

Every transaction pays a small fee to the computers that add it to the blockchain. On Ethereum it’s often called gas. The fee goes up when the network is busy and down when it’s quiet, and it’s paid in that network’s own coin, so you need a little ETH to move tokens on Ethereum.

A failed transaction can still cost a fee, because the network did the work of checking it.

Swaps

Trade one crypto for another, right from your wallet.

A swap trades one crypto for another, say ETH for USDC, without a traditional exchange in the middle. The price can move a little between the moment you see it and the moment the trade goes through. That gap is called slippage, and most apps let you set a limit on it.

Why prices move

Prices are whatever people agree on right now, and they swing.

Most crypto has no fixed value. Its price is whatever buyers and sellers agree on at that moment, so it moves with news, interest rates, big holders buying or selling, and plain mood. Large swings in a single day are normal.

Stablecoins are the exception by design, and even they can wobble if people stop trusting the company behind them. Nothing on this page is financial advice.

Who holds your keys

If a company holds your keys, it holds your crypto.

There are two ways to hold crypto, and the difference matters more than any feature.

  • Someone else holds the keys. On most exchanges, the company keeps the keys and you have an account with them. It feels like a bank: you can reset a password. But if the company freezes withdrawals or fails, your crypto is stuck with it. This is called custody.
  • You hold the keys. Your recovery phrase is yours alone, and no company can freeze it. You also carry all the responsibility. If you lose the words, no one can help. This is called self-custody.

Neither is wrong. Know which one you’re using.

Scams to know

If someone contacts you first about your wallet, assume it’s a scam.

  • Fake support. Someone reaches out first, by DM, email or a comment, offering to fix a problem. Real support never asks for your recovery words or for you to “verify” your wallet on a website.
  • Address poisoning. A scammer sends you a tiny amount from an address that looks almost like one you’ve used, hoping you’ll copy it from your history next time. Check the whole address, not just the first and last few characters.
  • “Double your coins.” Anyone promising to send back more than you send is stealing. Giveaways run by celebrities or companies are fake.
  • Approvals you didn’t read. Some sites ask you to approve access to your tokens. One signature can let them move everything. If you can’t tell what you’re signing, don’t sign it.

How to stay secure

Eight habits. Do these and you avoid almost every common way people lose crypto.

  • Write your 12 words on paper.Keep it somewhere safe and private. No screenshots, no notes app, no cloud.
  • Never share your words.Not with support, not with a friend, not with any website.
  • Check the whole address.Compare every character before you send, not just the first and last few.
  • Send a small test first.Moving a lot? Send a little, confirm it arrived, then send the rest.
  • Match the network.Make sure you and the receiver are on the same one.
  • Don’t sign what you can’t read.If an app can’t tell you in plain words what a request does, say no.
  • Lock your phone.Use a passcode and Face ID, and keep your phone updated.
  • Ignore anyone who messages you first.Real help doesn’t arrive uninvited in your DMs.

Glossary

Address
Where crypto is sent. Safe to share.
Blockchain
A shared, public record of who owns what.
Coin
The native currency of a blockchain, like BTC or ETH.
Gas
The fee for a transaction on Ethereum and networks like it.
Key
The secret that lets you move your crypto.
Network
The blockchain a transaction happens on.
Recovery phrase
12 or 24 words that are your key. Also called a seed phrase.
Slippage
How much a price can change during a swap.
Stablecoin
A token meant to stay at a fixed value, usually one dollar.
Swap
Trading one crypto for another.
Token
A crypto asset built on another blockchain, like USDC.
Wallet
An app that holds your keys, not your coins.
